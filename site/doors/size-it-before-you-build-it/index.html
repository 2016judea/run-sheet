Door 06 · Between buildings · sheet 7

Size it before you build it.

Put a price on an empty mile first. Let the size of the answer decide whether there is a project.

On your dock

Someone wants to build a system to cut deadhead. Before anyone draws a box, pull every tour for six weeks. Split the miles into loaded and empty. Put a price on an empty mile. Then split the empty miles by why they happened: repositioning a trailer, returning an empty, a driver running bobtail, a pool swap. For each cause, compare the cost of tours that had it against tours that did not. Some causes cost money. Some, it turns out, come with cheaper tours. Now you know where the money is, how much there is, and whether a dispatcher or a planning system put it there.

Why it is true

Sizing before designing sounds obvious and almost nobody does it, because the design is the fun part and the sizing might say no. But the sizing is also where the real finding hides. In a one-way network, where freight flows out but rarely back, deadhead is the base case, not a mistake. The trade associations put industry deadhead around one mile in six. When the empty legs are planned by several systems that each solve their own piece and never see each other, the long ones are not a driver problem. They are a seam between planners. Half of doing this well is being willing to come back and say the number is small.

try it What does your deadhead cost?

Industry deadhead runs around one mile in six. If yours is already well under that, what is left is probably a seam between planners, not a driver problem.

Monday

  1. For six weeks of tours, compute loaded miles, empty miles and invoiced linehaul. Divide. That is your price per empty mile.
  2. Bucket empty miles by cause and by which planner or dispatcher created them. Compare tour cost with and without each cause.
  3. Pull the long tail back to your own average, not to zero. Price that. If it is small, say so and stop.

The rule, and why

No design doc before a sizing doc. The sizing doc may kill the project, and that is the point.

A project that cannot survive its own arithmetic should die on paper, where it costs a week, not in production, where it costs a year.

The trap

The finding will usually indict the planning systems, not the people running them. Say that plainly. It is neither an accusation nor an excuse, and the moment it lands as either, nobody will fix the seam.
