Door 9 · How to work

Test it like a drug.

Randomize on and off by the hour. Measure the neighbors. Keep the nulls.

What it looks like on your floor

A new staffing rule goes live at one building. Every hour a coin is flipped: rule on, rule off. After four weeks the on hours and off hours are compared on the one metric that counts, packages out the door on time, and on everything next to it. The rule works. It goes to two more buildings and the result holds. Then it goes everywhere. Later the same rule is tried on a different kind of floor, with the same design, and after forty-odd tests across five buildings it does nothing. That result is written down and kept.

Why it is true

Labor is a shared resource, so a staffing change has side effects by construction. Feeding one role starves another, and the starved role is where the next miss comes from. A before-and-after comparison cannot separate your change from the weather, the volume and the manager. Randomizing by the hour at the same site can. Measure the output metric and its neighbors, read the result by root cause, and when the number does not move, publish that too. A null result, correctly explained, is often the better piece of knowledge. It told us where the rule works and where it cannot.

Monday

  1. Pick one change. Flip it on and off by the hour at one site for four weeks. Do not pick the hours.
  2. Measure the metric you care about, the metrics one step upstream and downstream, and the role you pulled labor from.
  3. Write the result down whether or not it moved, with the design, the dates and the caveats attached.

The rule, and why

No rollout without a randomized on/off at the site that proved it, and every null gets a write-up.

A change that only ever gets a before-and-after will always look like it worked, and the organization will scale the ones that did not.

The trap

Any metric conditioned on whether someone responded is confounded. Messages go out when a station is already in trouble, so the hours with responses are pre-selected for being bad hours. Do not report "the hours people followed the system went worse" as a finding about the people.
